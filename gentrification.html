The Line as Gentrification: The Privatization of Public Space and the Interruption of Belonging

"QUOTE"

The Brandy waiting line does not only affect those who are waiting in line. By privatizing the public space of the sidewalk, the institution of Brandy Melville extends its physical reach, demarcating its neighboring spaces for the comfort of those lining up – a comfort that I have established is only possible to those in proximity to whiteness, thinness, and youth or those willing to approximate proximity to these institutions. By transgressing its established physical boundaries of the store, the brand expands the boundaries of its exclusion into what was once public space, extending the reach of ‘the white world’ it is constructing and benefitting from to those beyond its consumer base. The Brandy waiting line in its power to align a Brandy Melville collective, also enacts the potential of “block[ing] rather than enabl[ing] action [...] becoming points that accumulate stress, or stress points” (Ahmed, 2006). And they are indeed stress points, as exhibited by the negative reactions and documentations of the phenomenon online (O’Neil, 2020; Moore, 2025; Bubridge-Izqui, 2025). Returning to O’Neil’s article, her questioning of the phenomenon of the line amidst the shutting down of local businesses illustrates that at the root of her frustration is the questioning of why Brandy Melville specifically attracts this level of social and temporal investment while other businesses do not. For O’Neil and other dissenters, the line is a transgressive display of how local Toronto businesses are neglected in the public sphere while racist, exclusionary international brands, not only occupy the dominant share of the market, but are also creating “new ways to profit from urban space” (Kern, 2022).

In her book Gentrification Is Inevitable and Other Lies, Leslie Kern demonstrates that gentrification manifests in subtler forms than the mere replacement of physical space. She writes that “in the context of gentrification, everyday life gets reshaped in many ways, big and small, and these have a significant impact on how people understand their place in the neighbourhood” (2022). Once more, the issue of belonging emerges. Brandy Melville’s recruitment of girls and women into belonging with whiteness, thinness, and youth is an explicit demonstration of Jaime Guzmán’s definition of gentrification as a “whiteness project”, whose goal is to “prepare urban space for white, wealthy inhabitants” (as cited in Kern, 2022). Toronto’s Queen West, the street that houses the only Brandy Melville location in the city, has been struggling against processes of gentrification since 1985 (CBC Archives, 2020). The street has transformed from the “shamelessly seedy (in the best way ever)” artistic hub of Toronto to “uber-trendy yet more commercial, one new coffee shop, yoga studio, glassy condo building, retail chain, and high-end boutique at a time” (Davis, 2018). With high turnover of businesses, ranging from the locally-owned to the international behemoths of Zara and H&M (De Boer, 2024), gentrification through the physical displacement of small businesses is not sufficient to ensure a brand’s survival in the commercial spaces they occupy. Through the recruitment into long waiting lines, brands like Brandy Melville can legitimize their survival in Toronto’s flippant consumer economy through the public demonstration of its value, a representation of their ‘right to the city’ by occupying urban spaces, and creating ways to profit off of them. The positive feedback loop of representation reinforces the waiting line as integral to institutions and corporations’ access to capital.

This comes at the cost of those who do not belong in the waiting line, the non-white, non-thin, non-young who previously considered this area their space for belonging, leaving “them feeling—and even treated—like outsiders” (Kern, 2022). I would argue that the reaction to these lines online is because waiting lines, and especially the Brandy waiting line explicitly aligned towards whiteness and capital, transgress the “”silent gentrification” [that] tiptoes in via the sensory norms of whiteness”(Kern, 2022), rather loudly gentrifying urban spaces, and thus loudly Othering – provoking the excluded Other to document, react, and engage with the waiting line. The desire to brand the lines as community-building opportunities is an attempt to naturalize this capitalist phenomenon (Kern, 2022), attempting to displace the public desire to accurately represent these waiting lines as gentrifying barriers to public participation in the city.
