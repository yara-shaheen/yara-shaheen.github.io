The Line as Recruitment: Brandy Melville as an Orientation Device Towards Capital and Whiteness

“Where something’s a trend, people tend to kind of follow the trend”

Tsai also identifies the emergence of Toronto waiting lines as a form of social contagion. “Where something’s a trend, people tend to kind of follow the trend” (Ferriera, 2025). The trend in the case of the Brandy waiting line is simultaneously the desired commodity and the line that leads towards it, as a spectacle of commodity (Roberts, 2003). Sarah Ahmed writes extensively about lines in Queer Phenomenology, dissecting their role in phenomenologically orientating us in the pursuit of joining a collective. She suggests that we “think about lines as an effect of how energy, time, and resources are ‘‘directed’’ toward an object” and that “it might be the very act of attention [...] that produces ‘‘a sense’’ of a collective or social group”. Through this, “the repetition of the act of following makes the line disappear from view as the point from which ‘‘we’’ emerge” (Ahmed, 2006). The waiting line as a phenomenon is one way individuals can align themselves with a collective, shaping their self-identity through the objects they orient themselves towards, determining which “we” one represents themselves with. In the previous section, I established how the women and girls of the Brandy waiting line are denied from the collective of ‘public space’, conditionally limited to private-public spaces. In this section, I aim to explore the other collective these populations are hoping to align themselves with - the institution and collective of Brandy Melville.

What defines the collective of Brandy Melville and why do these populations want to align themselves towards it? Lang (2024) details the brand’s explicit intention to remain ‘exclusive’. Remaining exclusive requires acts of exclusion to recruit those with the ‘Brandy aesthetic’ and repel those without. The brand’s exclusionary sizing model, their documented history of racist and sexist hiring and work conditions (for example, segregating white employees to the floor and racialized employees to the register or the stockroom), and their reposting of user-generated content from “girls that fit a certain aesthetic—young, thin and white, often blonde and usually with long hair” (Lang, 2024), limits the boundaries of the ‘Brandy aesthetic’ to a very particular white femininity. Thus, consumers of the brand participate in an aspirational consumerism, or “status-induced consumerism that channels self-expression through the marketplace” (Duffy, 2017). By wearing the clothes designed for and targeted towards young, thin, white women and girls, consumers are closer to representing themselves along the ideological and aesthetic lines of this hegemonic beauty standard. The adherence to “the pressure to be feminine and respectable” (Phadke et al., 2011) determines one’s access to other spaces, marking the act of waiting in line for Brandy Melville as an opportunity to potentially ‘move up’ and align oneself with other lines of whiteness and capital for the purposes of class mobility, social status, or cultural capital (Ahmed, 2006). In the article The Most Popular Girl in Soho Is a Brandy Melville Employee, the story of Allegra Pinkowitz is presented as one of the success stories for this aspirational alignment with Brandy Melville (Coleman, 2025). Her ability to ‘move up’ from consumer to influencer, tourist attraction, and waged worker through her alignment with the institution of Brandy Melville offers an example of where “the promise of eventual capital or future success” (Duffy, 2017) is fulfilled, raising the stakes for participation as aspirational labour, aspirational class mobility, and aspirational cultural capital.

The physical store acts as an orientation device, privatizing the public space surrounding it to orientate those in line towards the brand. Those seeking to belong to this collective are recruited, or interpellated, into the line establishing an affirming relationship between institution and individual to developing the “body of the institution” (Ahmed, 2006). For the Brandy Melville collective to deliver on its promise of fulfilling aspirational proximity to whiteness, thinness, and youth - lines that allow ‘moving up’ in class mobility and social status -, it must demonstrate its own proximity to these institutions, shaping itself “by the proximity of some bodies and not others” (Ahmed, 2006). This eliminates the myth of community-building associated with the waiting line; its purpose becomes the legitimization of the institution of Brandy Melville, and the oppressive institutions the brand aligns with to recruit into its line those who aspire to ‘move up’ and join a collective of belonging. By participating in the line, one not only joins the collective but “sign[s] up” for the institution, with the aspiration to move up “requir[ing] leaving one’s body behind” to blend into the “sea of whiteness” (Ahmed, 2006), the sea of whiteness describing both the line and the institution of Brandy Melville. In seeking to define oneself, those waiting in the Brandy waiting line are forced to assimilate into the ‘we’ of the collective while also representing the institution of the brand, recruiting others through their demonstration of respectability, desirability and modernity.

To attract this financially and culturally gainful crowd, the Brandy waiting line, in its transgression of public/private, must offer a level of safety to these women and girls, using a “range of tactics to keep out those perceived to be dangerous” (Phadke, 2011). The blurring between the function of the line as potential consumers and as a marketing spectacle blurs the definition of danger, extending beyond physical vulnerability towards financial and social vulnerability for the brand, deeming representations of who are “perceived to be dangerous” as those with the potential to harm the brand.

By identifying which bodies benefit their institution in proximity, Brandy Melville also demonstrates whose representation can be dangerous. The brand has repeatedly identified racialized and fat women and girls as not only excluded from the target audience, but as ‘dangers’ to the brand. A former employee reported that he was instructed to “fire girls if they were too heavy or Black” (Horton, 2024), showcasing an institutional acknowledgement that these girls were threatening the “one size fits most” representation of femininity that the brand relies on to recruit aspiring consumers. The demarcation of the public space occupied by the Brandy waiting line as exclusionary to fat and Black girls and women inherently alienates them from engaging with the brand by creating a space that makes them uncomfortable and highly visible in the Brandy ‘sea of whiteness’.

Yet, the temptation to align oneself with whiteness, thinness, and youth through consumption is powerfully alluring, especially with proven cases of success populating the feeds of the racialized women and girls who are eager to ‘move up’. When individuals from these populations phenomenologically align themselves towards the institution of Brandy Melville, amidst the curated discomfort meant to alienate them, then they must ‘leave behind’ much more of their bodies “by approximating the habitus of the white bourgeois body” (Ahmed, 2006), the bodies of their white bourgeois counterparts that have recruited them into the line, bodies that were specifically targeted to be comfortable in this space. This phenomenological contract goes beyond the hours-long wait, especially for the women and girls who recognize the waged potential of fitting the ‘Brandy aesthetic’. It continues as the consumers embody the clothes for their followers, in the hopes of being reposted by the brand or engaged with by the larger collective that lines up towards it.

The insidiousness of this requirement is also rooted in the lack of any guarantees for these women and girls. While they voluntarily minimize their bodies to fit the ‘Brandy aesthetic’ of whiteness and thinness and spectaclize themselves as commodity for the recruitment of others, Brandy Melville can enjoy the value their bodies provide and exploit them for further profit while the women and girls remain unpaid and unprotected (Duffy, 2017). The betrayal of the Brandy waiting line and the false promises of belonging into its collective serve as a multi-layered policing tool; firstly on the consumer level, as she self-polices in order to be permitted into the line, then on the level of the line itself, as an extension of the institution of Brandy Melville, phenomenologically reinforcing who is and is not allowed to be comfortable and belong, and finally on the level of the passers-by, who document this phenomenon with anger and confusion, contributing to the hypervisibility of the line. These processes of policing create a positive feedback loop, reinforced by lines as “performative: they depend on the repetition of norms and conventions [...] created as an effect of this repetition” (Ahmed, 2006). The visual representation of the line becomes further identified with the brand, reaffirming the importance of policing who is represented in the line, making the barriers of access to these lines stricter in their codes of whiteness and thinness, normalizing and extending the concept of whiteness as the object at the end of line that is capable of fulfilling social, cultural, and financial capital. The promises of a sense of collective belonging have only served to minimize and oppress the women and girls eager to participate in the collective, forced to adhere to respectable standards of white femininity.
