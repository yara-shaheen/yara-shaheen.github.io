Introduction

"do people not have jobs in toronto?"

This is the question Alice Burbidge-Izqui (2025) overlays upon an aerial view of the long waiting line for an Innisfree pop-up. The Korean skincare and beauty brand’s pop-up Matcha Mart is one of many Toronto attractions that have garnered waiting lines, and those documenting the lines, that spill into the streets and bend around the corners. Gelateria and cafe, Mizzica (@naddie.baddie, 2025), Tim Horton’s latest activation in Eaton Centre (Kharoud, 2025), any Holiday/Seasonal Market (Sagetha, 2025) or ‘cheap eats’ deal (Felisha, 2025) - the list of attractions that fall under this category of ‘trendy waiting lines’ vary in permanence, offering, and location but have quickly become an identifiable Toronto phenomenon, evoking varying reactions and engagement.

The TikTok by Burbidge-Izqui (2025) contributes to the reactionary discourse that leads with a negative affect of confusion, captioning their videos with “People in Toronto will wait in line for anything” (Connie, 2022) or the “Toronto line waiting epidemic” (Moore, 2025). On the other hand, food reviewers and lifestyle influencers confirm and accentuate this phenomenon with restaurant recommendations that are “worth waiting in line for” (Jeyan, 2025), with one user waiting up to four hours for a popular Toronto brunch spot (@jastodoc, 2025). The spectacle of what Moore (2025) labels the “Toronto line waiting epidemic” has become an integral part of defining the city’s identity, inspiring a positive portrayal of it amongst similar articles in digital publications like Toronto Star, (Warren, 2019), BlogTO (Juniper, 2023) and Now Toronto (Ferreira, 2025). The Now Toronto article entitled: “Why do Torontonians love to line up? Expert says there’s many reasons, including making friends”, highlights the interviewee Dr. Daniel Tsai’s answer that most closely ties to Debord’s theory that culture, and the spectacles that define it, are “the search for lost unity” (Roberts, 2003), mirroring the framing in the other articles for the waiting lines as a community-building opportunity.

Tsai’s other reasons emerge from his position as a Professor at the Ted Rogers School of Management, citing ‘social contagion’ and a sense of consumer urgency to try viral experiences and products. He also states that the long waiting line serves as ‘social proof’ for brands’ legitimacy, serving as a “phenomenon of viral marketing” (Ferreira, 2025). I would like to expand on these propositions, arguing that the phenomenon goes beyond marketing for individual brands, or even for Toronto as a city. I propose that through recruitment based on false promises of community-building and collective belonging, the trendy waiting lines of Toronto act as a disruptive act of gentrification, phenomenologically and ideologically enforcing an alignment to capital and whiteness as a spectacle of commodity, as defined by David Roberts (2003). I aim to focus my exploration of this social phenomenon on the waiting lines for the Brandy Melville store in Toronto, due to their audience of primarily female-presenting consumers and their highly-publicized racist and misogynistic working conditions and marketing practices.

Through a reliance on Brooke Erin Duffy’s (2017) (Not) Getting Paid to Do what You Love: Gender, Social Media, and Aspirational Work and Shilpa Phadke et al.’s (2011) Why Loiter?: Women and Risk on Mumbai Street, I will establish first the aspirational motivations of engaging in public space that recruit women and girls into the waiting line, and the hidden standards of femininity and respectability that dictate their levels of involvement. I will further break down the myth of these waiting lines as ‘community-building’ by utilizing Sara Ahmed’s (2006) exploration of the policing phenomenological experience of waiting in line, with its intersections to race and capital from her book Queer phenomenology: Orientations, Objects, Others. I conclude by demonstrating that these lines are in fact community-disrupting through their privatization of public spaces, urging a connection to Leslie Kern’s (2022) widened definition of gentrification in Gentrification Is Inevitable and Other Lies.
