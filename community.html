The Line as Community-Building Theatre: Spectaclizing the Female Longing for Public Participation

"one size fits all"

Brandy Melville is known for many things; its “one-size-fits-most” philosophy, the many reports of racism, sexism, and toxic work environments, and its perpetuation of wasteful, neocolonial fast fashion practices (Horton, 2024; McMenanim, 2024; Lang, 2024). Recently, the Italian fast fashion brand has also become notorious for its long waiting lines, as observed in major American and Canadian cities like Washington (Taylor, 2025), New York (Druckman, 2025), and Toronto (O’Neil, 2020b). O’Neil’s article chronicling what I will refer to as the ‘Brandy waiting line’ was published in September, 2020, shortly after retail stores were allowed to reopen post-pandemic (O’Neil, 2020a). She questions why “all these skinny 13yr old white girls” (Vlasov, 2020, as cited in O’Neil, 2020b) are waiting for up to four hours for a highly-controversial brand, while local businesses neighboring Toronto’s only Brandy Melville location are forced to shut down due to "challenges resulting from the pandemic” (2020b). In the “child poverty capital of Canada” (Raveendran, 2024), the ever-present line of tweens and teenagers along Queen West returns us to the original question posed by user Burbidge-Iziqui (2025) of who has time to wait in line, with the addendum of ‘to spend an average of 20 CAD on a baby tee’.

In this case, the answer is Brandy Melville’s meticulously-curated cult following of mainly white, high-school-aged girls and Gen Z women that are trend-aware, very online, and able to fit in the mostly XS/S-sized clothing (Horton, 2024). While the long waiting lines speak to the brand’s effective marketing, the hours-long commitment to stand in line speaks to a secondary, historied motivation to occupy public space. In Why Loiter?, Phadke et al. (2011) demonstrate an inherent desire from women and girls to participate in the public spaces of the city, and the many obstacles of respectability, sexist infrastructure, and concerns for safety that deny access to the fulfillment of this desire. The mere act of achieving visibility in public spaces for teenage girls and young women is seen as ‘courting risk’, transgressive for blurring the defined “binary of masculine/public and feminine/private” (Duffy, 2017). The one locale that both Duffy and Phadke et al. explore as a refuge from the hyper-visibility of occupying public spaces is the department store or mall, respectively. Phadke et al. (2011) write that “women’s body language in malls demonstrates a sense of belonging that is not really visible in other kinds of public spaces”. This sense of belonging can be seen in the confidence of the girls occupying the Brandy waiting line (Druckman, 2025), and could easily be mistaken as a reclamation of women and girls’ right to the city. Indeed, the reactionary confusion that this phenomenon spurs affirms what a peculiar sight it is to see this population occupying public space so unabashedly and dominantly. Occupants in the line, admittedly in a comedic tone, post about how “it’s the one who waits in the Brandy Melville line with you” (Katherine, 2024), speaking to an air of solidarity in the face of the struggle of waiting in this ‘public’ space.

Phadke et al. (2011) remind us, however, that this theatre of transgression, is predicated on “a demonstration of their capacity to buy,” with the illusion of community and safety that it permits extending only to “keep[ing] middle-class women safe”. The dependence on financial capacity in exchange for goods abolishes the notion that the mall is a public space, placing it in the realm of the privatized public space. Similarly, while the young women and girls lining up on the sidewalks might shallowly appear as an act of loitering, the occupation of this public space with the explicit intent of spending money at a singular store privatizes the occupied space for the fashion brand, enabling them to expand their physical real estate for free, and moreover, for additional capital gain acquired off of the spectacle that those waiting in line provide.

Despite its infamous reputation, Brandy Melville continues to hold a prominent place in youth culture through effective marketing strategies, including the ‘organic’ marketing strategy of user-generated content. In her review of Brandy Hellville and The Cult of Fast Fashion, Cady Lang (2024) cites the brand as “an early adapter of the influencer marketing model”, with their tendency to repost consumers’ shopping hauls to their 3+ million Instagram followers encouraging users to consume their products in haul-worthy amounts, embody them for their audience, and post it online with no guarantee of compensation or exposure. The spectaclization of the Brandy waiting line introduces another element of the shopping process to profit on, with users legitimizing the spectacle by posting advice on the best times to shop for shorter lines (Careri, 2024), labelling the security guard managing the entrance as the “the most important man in all of Toronto” (Balandina, 2024), and passers-by contributing to the digital archival, interrogation, and popularization of the phenomenon. The physical act of lining up, and capturing this physical commitment to the brand, acknowledges the unwaged labor taking place, adding an element of authenticity to this ‘peacocking’, firmly establishing the waiting line as a diffuse spectacle of the commodity, justifying “capitalism by elevating consumption to an aesthetic experience” (Roberts, 2003) and exemplifying the few ways women are permitted to engage in the public sphere; through “consumption-oriented visibility” (Duffy, 2017).

Through this reading, Tsai’s (Ferreira, 2025) proposition of the line as ‘social proof’ seems an accurate justification for why Torontonians are lining up, at least in this case, serving as an act of ‘labor of devotion’, or “women actively promot[ing] their favorite brands to other women” (Duffy, 2017). Thus, the Brandy waiting line “offers the illusion of loitering while insidiously reinforcing gender roles and normative sexuality and class codes,” providing enough of a promise of community and thrill of transgression to seem appealing, while fulfilling the requirement that women’s access to public space must have the “demonstration of purpose” for going out and “a demonstration of their capacity to buy” (Phadke et al., 2011). The girls and women in the Brandy waiting line are exploited for their desire to safely participate in public space, only permitted access if they themselves become a spectacle of commodity, exchanging the social capital of the visibility of their bodies for conditional public participation.
